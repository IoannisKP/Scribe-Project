01 · The idea

Recording a conversation shouldn't mean handing it to a stranger

Every mainstream transcription tool uploads your audio to a company's servers, and most join the call as a visible bot so everyone can see something is listening. For a job candidate, a client, or a doctor's visit, that isn't acceptable.

Scribe captures both sides of a conversation on your Mac, transcribes it there, and sends nothing anywhere without explicit consent. No bot. No account. No servers.

The whole product is a single promise: your words stay yours.

02 · My role

I wrote the specifications. The AI wrote the code. Neither of us could do the other's job.

The AI couldWrite 2,000 lines of Swift, run 283 tests in twelve seconds, measure memory, bisect its own commits, and correct my technical wording against Apple's own documentation.
Only I couldDecide what the product should be, then put on headphones, record thirty seconds, and check that what landed on disk was real.

I never read the code. I read the reports, pushed on the parts that sounded too smooth, and verified the claims with my own senses. That division turned out to be the entire lesson of the project.

It's worth being fair about the agent. It refused to click an irreversible delete on my behalf and asked me to do it myself. It told me, correctly, that a verification method I had specified was invalid because the two quantities I was comparing didn't measure the same thing. When one of its own fixes improved one model and broke another, it noticed and reverted before I saw the result. This is not a story about a machine being bad at its job.

03 · The process

Specify. Build. Verify. Repeat.

01SpecifyRequirements in plain English, down to the edge cases and the things not to build. Acceptance criteria written before any code.
02BuildThe agent wrote the code, wrote its own tests, ran them, and reported back, usually in minutes.
03VerifyI checked every claim in a way a machine couldn't fake. Record thirty seconds. Open the folder. Play the file. Read the byte count.

The loop is the method. Anything the agent could test, it tested well. Everything that went badly wrong went wrong in the gap between what can be automated and what has to be witnessed.

04 · The findings

Four things every passing test missed

Each of these was reported as finished. Each passed a full green test suite. Each was caught by recording thirty seconds and checking what actually landed on disk.

Reported

Live transcription complete. All 168 tests passing.

Actual

Zero transcripts had ever been written to disk.

The app had never saved a transcript

Text appeared as I spoke, corrected itself mid-sentence, labelled who was talking. Then you pressed stop and every word was discarded. The transcription worked. The file-writing worked. They had simply never been connected, and no test covered the join. I found it by looking in a folder for a file that wasn't there.

Reported

Both tracks captured. Timing accurate to 346 samples.

Actual

The first 2 to 4.5 seconds of the other person, never recorded.

Two seconds of the other person, gone

A timing figure was off by 21 thousandths of a second. Chasing that meaningless discrepancy meant comparing it against the real length of both files, and that gap was two seconds. The microphone started first; the second stream took seconds to come up behind it. The "hi, can you hear me" window was simply absent from every recording.

Reported

“Pinned.” Every time, instantly.

Actual

Nothing written, and a data-loss bug underneath it.

A confirmation that confirmed nothing

A keystroke marks an important moment mid-meeting. The app said Pinned and saved nothing: the message fired on the keypress rather than on a successful write. The cause was worse than the symptom: a background process was periodically writing an out-of-date copy of the session file over the current one, erasing anything saved since it last looked.

Reported

Two isolated tracks. Recording successful.

Actual

Two copies of the same audio, then an empty track, then one at double speed.

Both microphones recorded the same sound

With headphones on, the two files should have nothing in common. They were identical. The microphone had attached itself to a virtual device picking up the computer's own output. Fixing that broke Bluetooth, which advertises one audio format for a few seconds before settling on another, so half the second track was discarded during conversion and played back twice as fast.

Four of those six sessions reported success. The whole table came from one command that reads each file's header and prints how long it claims to be, a check that takes ten seconds and would have caught both defects on the day they appeared.

05 · The counterweight

Then I was confidently wrong about 44 bytes

I flagged a defect: two audio files came back at 44 bytes each, nothing but a header, no audio at all. I wrote up what had happened, what to investigate, and the fix: the app should fail loudly whenever a track comes back empty.

What I was certain ofTwo empty files means capture is broken.
What was trueNothing had been playing. 44 bytes was exactly correct.

Worse, the fix I had specified would have broken every legitimate recording where the other person simply doesn't speak. A silent room is a valid meeting. What saved it was that I hadn't sent the report yet. I was still collecting evidence, and the evidence didn't hold.

I had been exactly as confident as the AI, and exactly as wrong.

Every report after that started the same way: here is precisely what I observed, here is what I think it means, tell me if I'm wrong before you change anything. Directing an AI means holding your own assumptions to the standard you hold the machine's.

06 · The design

Fifteen seconds of silence is a design problem

Running the models on a laptop instead of a datacentre has a price: about fifteen seconds pass before the first word appears, and during continuous speech a paragraph can take thirty seconds to settle. That isn't going away, so the interface had to make the wait honest rather than hide it behind a spinner.

Notes firstTyping is the only thing that responds instantly, so it gets the room. The transcript is a column you can collapse.
Two live metersDriven separately from transcription. Before a word appears you can see both sides are being captured.
Name the wait“First text in about 16 seconds,” calculated from the model you chose. A wait you were told about is a fact.

Two tracks make attribution a fact, not a guess

Because your voice and everyone else's are captured separately and never mixed, the app doesn't infer who spoke, it knows. Most tools guess this from a single combined recording.

07 · The data promise

Every recording is just a folder

Open it in Finder and there's the audio, the transcript, your notes, and the summary. Separate files, in formats anything can read. No database to escape from. If Scribe disappears tomorrow, your recordings still open in TextEdit.

What you write is never edited by what a machine produces. The common failure of these tools is that the generated version quietly becomes the thing you keep. Four separate files makes that structural, not a promise.

08 · The refusals

Most of the product decisions were refusals

No automatic recordingStarting from a calendar entry is convenient, and an app that records without being asked can't stay installed.
No voice enrollmentIdentifying people by voice works well. It also means storing voiceprints of people who never agreed to it.
No AI chat panelA fifth surface that blurs the line between what you wrote and what a machine wrote. That line staying visible is the whole point.
No cloud transcriptionSending audio to a third party is a far bigger step than sending text, and local accuracy was already good enough.
No invented titlesSessions name themselves from their content. When a session is too short to name honestly, the app keeps the date. A wrong guess is worse than no guess.
No silent failuresIf a track records nothing while you're speaking, the app has to say so during the recording, not report success and let you find out later.

Each of these took longer to argue through than it would have taken to build. That ratio is most of product work, and the least visible part of it.

09 · Where it stands

It works. I use it. And it isn't finished.

Two-track capture, twelve local transcription models measured for accuracy and memory before being offered, live notes with pinning, file import, and summaries from Claude, OpenAI, DeepSeek or a model running on your own machine.

Not done: speaker separation for group calls, and distribution. For now it runs from source on my own machine. This is a working app I use daily, not a mockup, and it is thirteen days old. The last bug in this write-up was found the morning I wrote it.

10 · The takeaway

The code wasn't the hard part

AI writes code faster and more fluently than I could ever learn to, and it will keep getting better. That is not the interesting part, and it isn't where the risk lives.

The problem was never bad code. It was code that looked finished: it compiled, passed 283 of its own tests, and reported success while throwing every transcript away.

Nothing automated caught any of that. It was caught by a person deciding what the product was supposed to do, and then checking, physically, whether it did. Record thirty seconds. Open the folder. Play the file. Read the number.

That is the shape of the work now: someone has to hold the vision, write the specification precisely enough to be built against, and then audit the result against reality rather than against a green checkmark. The machine can't do the last part, because it can't hear.

What this project shows A designer who doesn't write code took a product from an idea to a working app in thirteen days, by writing precise specifications, making every product call, and refusing to trust a passing test suite. The verification loop wasn't overhead around the real work. It was the work.
